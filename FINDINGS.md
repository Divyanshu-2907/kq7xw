# What January told us about wind forecasts

A one-page brief for the day-ahead desk. Every figure comes from Elexon's own
published data for January 2026 — 1,488 half-hourly delivery periods — judged
strictly on what had been published before the auction gate closed.

---

## 1. At the gate, the forecast is typically wrong by 2,145 MW

Measured at the 09:20 London gate, across the whole month, the average miss was
**2,145 MW** against average wind output of **11,581 MW**. Half the time the miss
was under 1,795 MW. One period in ten missed by more than **4,275 MW**.

That is the number to hold in mind whenever a forecast is quoted to you as a
single figure. It is not a bad forecast — it is a forecast of weather, 24 to 40
hours out — but a plan built on the headline number alone is wrong by about two
gigawatts on an average day.

## 2. At 90% confidence, you could have counted on 9,060 MW

This is the output that matters. Taking the forecast and subtracting the error it
has historically made at that notice period, the figure you could have committed
to with 90% confidence averaged **9,060 MW** — about **4,373 MW below** the
headline forecast.

**It worked.** Wind actually came in below that plan in **10.3% of periods**,
against the 10% the method promised. The uncertainty estimate is trustworthy, and
it was built using only errors from earlier weeks, never from the week being
planned.

Other settings, same month:

| Certainty you want | Plan on | Actually fell short |
|---|---|---|
| 50% (coin flip) | 11,774 MW | 53.4% of periods |
| 80% | 9,939 MW | 19.6% |
| **90%** | **9,060 MW** | **10.3%** |
| 95% | 8,338 MW | 6.9% |

## 3. Your auction choice is worth more than your forecast timing

The forecast is republished eight times a day, but the 09:30 publication misses
the 09:20 EPEX hourly gate by **ten minutes**. Every day, the freshest forecast
you can legally use at that gate is the one from 07:30.

Waiting for the later N2EX gate at 09:50 gets you a two-hour fresher forecast and
improves accuracy by just **19 MW**. Trading into the half-hourly auction that
closes at **15:30** instead improves it by **160 MW — 7.5%**. If volume can move
to the later auction, that is worth more than anything the forecaster will do for
you this year.

## 4. One day caused the month's worst exposure

Every one of the ten worst periods in January fell on **12 January**, between
midnight and 05:30. At 01:00 the forecast promised **20,942 MW** and wind
delivered **14,048 MW** — short by **6,894 MW**, about a third of what was
expected, with 17 hours of notice.

It was not a mistimed weather front. Shifting the forecast by up to three hours
in either direction removes only about 5% of the error, so the forecast had the
shape of the day roughly right and the **level** badly wrong. Planning for a
timing error would not have protected you; planning for a level error would.

## 5. The quiet hours are the risky ones

Error is worst around **05:00 (2,541 MW)** and best around **21:00 (1,886 MW)** —
a 35% swing across the day, and the forecast leans high overnight in particular.
Error also grows with the forecast itself: when the forecast promises more than
14 GW, the average miss is **2,672 MW**. That is where most of January sat, and
it is where overnight exposure concentrates.

---

## One caveat you should know about

The two Elexon datasets do not line up on level. The forecast series runs about
**1.16 times** the metered output, consistently. Dividing it by that single
number (a median ratio of **1.1603**) removes **46.5%** of the error at a
24-hour notice period across January — and **42.2%** of the 2,145 MW figure in
point 1, which is measured at the tighter 09:20 gate. Either way, roughly half
of what looks like forecast error is a level difference, not uncertainty.

**We can measure it precisely but cannot yet explain it, and we would rather say
so than guess.** Two things are happening together. There is a steady gap of
about 0.6 to 1.0 GW at every wind level, which looks like the two datasets
counting different wind farms. And there is a much bigger shortfall at high wind:
metered output never exceeded **17.8 GW** all month, while the forecast reached
**21.2 GW**. The windiest 40% of periods account for **72%** of the total gap,
which is what curtailment or a metering ceiling looks like. Fitting either
explanation to the data gives almost identical accuracy, so January alone cannot
separate them.

None of this undermines the confidence figures in point 2. Those learn whatever
level difference is present from history and correct for it automatically, which
is why the 90% plan came in at 10.3%. It does mean the raw gap should not be read
as a judgement on the forecaster.

## What we would recommend

1. **Stop planning on the headline forecast.** Use the 90% figure as the volume
   you treat as firm, and buy the difference.
2. **Check whether volume can move to the 15:30 auction.** It is the single
   largest accuracy gain available.
3. **Size overnight positions more cautiously** than daytime ones.
4. **Let us get curtailment or capacity data** so we can separate the two
   explanations for the level offset, and extend to twelve months before these
   bands are used for a summer position.
